**danicuki** (2026-03-16T13:00:00.000Z) [msgid:$dUGvZtZC1cY-JOtrsLsV88VKMmNQeG4mekKavvimONI]:
I think we may be facing a bit of a chicken-and-egg problem in the review process:

• We need knowledgeable reviewers for JAM submissions, but currently most people with enough technical context are also JAM implementers.
• Ideally reviewers should be impartial, but the pool of people familiar enough with JAM is still very small.
• In the future, teams that pass milestones could potentially become reviewers, but the first milestones still require an initial set of qualified reviewers.
• Even then, because of the prize structure, some conflicts of interest may still exist when existing teams review other submissions.

Maybe the solution is not trying to find perfectly impartial reviewers (which might be unrealistic in such a small expert ecosystem), but designing a process that mitigates conflicts.

For example:
• multiple reviewers per milestone
• conflict-of-interest declarations and recusal when appropriate
• majority-based decisions instead of a single reviewer
• public review reports explaining the reasoning

Another possibility could be aligning incentives — for example reviewers staking some DOT and reviews being challengeable — but that might be more complex to implement.

Curious to hear how others think this could best be handled.

**danicuki** (2026-03-16T21:53:00.000Z) [msgid:$7upV_qPnwvyeTWsixRLAscd5jf13GmIcXu2zkIRaISU]:
Right, and that’s where the deadlock seems to appear.

The people who have enough technical context to properly evaluate a JAM implementation are mostly the implementers themselves. But if implementers cannot look at other teams’ code due to the prize rules, then the remaining reviewers may not have enough familiarity with the protocol to judge correctness.

So we end up with a structural problem: the people who can evaluate the code cannot see it, and the people who can see it may not be able to evaluate it properly.
