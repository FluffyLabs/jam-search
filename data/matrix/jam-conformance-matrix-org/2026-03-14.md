That is a very different model than JAM uses. JAM doesn't need any special hardware, and it depends mostly on determinism and game theory to make sure people don't cheat. It doesn't depend on any centralized point of failure for security. TEE does, as that depends on the big centralized hardware companies not being malicious, and it also depends on the implementations (which are relatively complex) being pretty much bug-free. So philosophically it's probably not a very great fit for JAM.

And, of course, it doesn't really help with all of the other problems that decentralized GPU compute has (which, TEE or not, you would need to solve somehow).

**jan** (2026-03-14T14:52:00.000Z) [msgid:$zC9CQC5ofUnI0E-oZ74EH9dMB3dl7Zf3ZJvlsbN-yaE]:
It's not only ordering of threads. On a low level things like e.g. exact FP semantics (e.g. in various corner cases) are not guaranteed to be the same between different hardware revisions, or even different driver versions AFAIK. And there's a _ton_ of behavior that is, essentially, undefined (e.g. open Nvidia's PTX docs, search for "undefined" and see how many matches you'll get). In practice that usually doesn't matter, but it starts to matter when you combine "100% determinism" and "permissionless" as your requirements. And for many practical workloads you _want_ to use functionality which doesn't guarantee determinism, be it run-to-run or simply across different hardware generations/vendors (e.g. for ML workloads you most definitely want to use the tensor cores in your GPU, otherwise you're leaving a ton of performance on the table).

With enough engineering and tradeoffs you could make it work, maybe? It also heavily depends on the kinds of workloads we'd expect people to run. Running a bunch of expensive ZK math really fast? Might make practical sense. Training a non-toy transformer? No sane person would use it if it's not reasonably competitive with traditional cloud-rented hardware.
